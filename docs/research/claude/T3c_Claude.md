How it was researched: the agent worked in four parallel passes, one per plant group, plus one on climate:
- trees and vine;
- field crops;
- scrub, wet ground, giant reed and the grasses;
- the plants known only from paintings, and the plants that must not appear.

Sarpaki's 1987 Sheffield thesis on the plants of the West House was read in full from White Rose eTheses. Its rotated tables (the Santorini crop calendar from informants in Akrotiri village, 1982–83, the yields and crop processing) and two doubtful passages were read as page images. For the living species the passes read:
- *Flora Europaea* treatments, through Plazi;
- Halácsy's *Conspectus Florae Graecae* (1900–04), which records the plants of Thera from Heldreich's flora of 1899;
- the Greek checklist (Dimopoulos et al. 2013, *Englera* 31);
- the European Atlas of Forest Tree Species;
- one year of phenology in a lowland Cretan phrygana (Bergmeier and Matthäs 1996);
- phenology studies of olive and table grape on Crete, Santorini vintages, figs in Aegean Turkey, and trials of Greek barley and wheat landraces near Athens;
- Theophrastus and Hesiod in public-domain translations.

The Flora of Greece web portal, eFloras, Kew's Plants of the World Online, Flora of Israel Online and ResearchGate refused automated access throughout.

## How this lane reads the grades, places and months (read first)
- **Grades follow the brief, read for this lane.**
  - **A** is a find from Thera before the eruption that shows a plant was there, or shows its size or form: seeds, leaves, charcoal, timber, impressions, the olive trees buried standing, the Therasia olive shrub, the bronze sickles. The measurement is A. A specialist's reading of it (pruning, uprooting, brushing the crop) is B.
  - **B** is a Theran wall painting or pot (shown, not found: the plant itself is B at most), or an Akrotiri find whose reading or phase is debated (the flax and lupin of Delta room Δ1a, whose sample is labelled Middle Cycladic).
  - **C** is everything else. That covers every modern description of a living species and every month from a flora or a phenology study, which describe today's plant in today's climate; each such row says "modern" and names its region. It also covers the Santorini crop calendar of 1982–83, Hesiod and Theophrastus, the modern trials of landraces, and this lane's own INFERENCE.
- **Places.** Every row names the region its figure comes from. The southern Aegean is preferred. Lowland south-west Crete is the main analogue for wild plants, Crete for olive and vine, and Attica for the cereal trials. Aegean Turkey, Cyprus, Italy, Spain and the Balearics, Israel, Iran, central Europe and the United States are named as such where they had to stand in.
- **Months** are today's calendar months, at low altitude. They are C for 1628 BCE, and no Aegean month names are attested (`docs/WORLD_CLOCK.md`).
  - Thera is drier than the Cretan sites: 362 mm a year in 1894–1914 (#2).
  - The climate of the time differed little from today's: Late Bronze Age air at most about 1 °C warmer (#5), and late Holocene summers in the east a little wetter than now (#7).
  - Modern Crete has warmed 0.36–0.40 °C per decade over the observation period (#25), so today's flowering dates may run early for the past (#91).
  - INFERENCE: dates for 1628 BCE may differ by days or weeks, not by seasons.
  - The world's opening year has its own question. Aniakchak II erupted in Alaska in 1628 BCE and trees show frost rings in 1627 (`WORLD_CLOCK`, X1#17, #79). Whether a cold summer touched Aegean harvests is not known.
- **Ancient against modern forms.** Crops were landraces and fruit trees were unimproved. Where a source measures the difference, it is given (Section 3; Assumption T3c-3). Where none does, the row says so.
- **Species most likely.** Where the find gives only a genus or a "type" (thyme, savory, oak, tamarisk, juniper, *Pistacia*, pear), the species named is this lane's INFERENCE (C). It rests on what grows on Thera today (Heldreich 1899, via Halácsy) and in the Cyclades (*Englera* 31).
- **Marks.** "INFERENCE" is this lane's reasoning, always C. "[snippet only]" means the fact comes from a search result's text and the page did not open: C at most. "Could not verify" means no opened source gives it.
- **Names.** Plant names are modern scientific and common names. No ancient plant name is known: Linear A is undeciphered. Greek words such as *arakas*, *fava*, *zevgari*, *kouloura* and *kladeftiko* are modern.

## Section 1: Summary

- **The year on low ground** (modern Cretan analogue, C).
  - Most of Santorini's rain falls from November to March, 46–73 mm a month; June to August have 2 mm each (#2).
  - The ground greens with the rains of late October or November, and is greenest in December to February (#222, #223).
  - Flowering peaks from the end of March to about 20 April, mostly annuals, and the ground looks yellow (#224).
  - Annuals wither from late April, and the summer-dry look prevails by mid-May (#225, #226).
  - From early July to mid-September nothing is in main flower. The colours run from yellow-brown to grey-brown (#227, #228).
  - On Thera itself, 64 species were in flower in January 1992 (#232).
- **Late May, the default day** (all modern analogues, C).
  - Most low ground is dry and yellow to grey-brown, with late flowers if early May was wet (#226, #230).
  - Barley stands ripe and losing its green. It is cut in June on Thera today and at the end of May near Athens (#111, #137, #147).
  - *Lathyrus clymenum* pods are browning and the plants are being pulled. Pea fields are already cleared, and threshing is under way (#112, #114, #119).
  - On low ground the olives have finished flowering and are setting tiny fruit (#24). Vines are in pale green flower (#48). Figs are in full leaf, with the early crop (brebas) near ripe and the main crop just showing (#39, #42).
  - Thyme is opening its first flowers (#173, #175) and savory is in pink flower (#179). Thorny burnet is changing to small summer leaves, its fruit ripening red (#185, #186; the colour from a weak web flora, #189).
  - New giant-reed canes about 1 m tall rise among last year's (#217).
  - The white lily is in flower if it was grown (#258, #261). No crocus shows (#255). The sea daffodil is in leaf but not yet in flower (#270).
- **The olive, the one tree seen in place** (A).
  - Two trees were buried standing near Fira, 9 m apart, with branch moulds 1–3 m above the base (#8, #10, #11).
  - A trunk 2 m long is reported under the pumice (#33), and a shrub with stems of 2.5 cm or less on Therasia (#12).
  - The charcoal is cut branches 1.5–5 cm thick, read as pruning or pollarding (B) (#14, #15).
  - Modern olives (C) grow up to 15 m. The leaves are 20–80 × 5–15 mm, dark grey-green above and silvery beneath. The fruit is 10–35 mm, green, then red-purple, then black, and is picked from November to December (#19–#21, #26).
- **Fruit trees by month** (C).
  - Fig: leaves 10–20 cm and palmate; bare from late November–December to March (#37, #38, #41).
  - Vine: budburst in late March, flowering from mid-May to early June, ripening in July and August on Crete (#48). The Santorini vintage averages 10 August today (#49); village memory puts it in September (#51).
  - Almond: pink-white bloom from February to March, on Spanish data (#54, #55).
- **Wild trees and shrubs** (species unknown, C for form).
  - Kermes oak, which grows on Santorini today, is a prickly evergreen bush up to 2 m (#32, #58).
  - The deciduous oak is unidentified. Downy oak grows 15–20 m and leafs out in March (#61, #62).
  - The pines are Aleppo or Turkish pine, 20 m and more (#65–#67).
  - Phoenician juniper grows 5–8 m. It is low and wind-shaped on sea cliffs, with dark red cones (#71, #72).
  - Tamarisk grows 4–15 m, with pink or white sprays (#76). Terebinth and mastic are covered by #83–#86.
  - Leaf fall, and the flowering of most of these, could not be verified (Section 3.5).
- **Fields by the Santorini calendar of 1982–83** (C).
  - Barley is manured in late August, sown in October and harvested in June (#111).
  - *L. clymenum* is sown from October to January among the vines. Its shoot tips are picked as greens about 15 March, and it is pulled in May–June (#112, #113).
  - Pea and *L. cicera* are harvested in April (#114).
  - The ground gets one ard ploughing (#128), and the stubble is grazed through the summer (#116).
  - Vines are ploughed three times and picked in September (#51).
- **Straw height.** No Bronze Age measurement exists.
  - Barley on Thera today barely reaches 30–40 cm (#109). Greek barley landraces stand 66–81 cm near Athens (#138).
  - Greek wheat landraces stand 118–159 cm, against 84–104 cm for modern varieties (#140, #141).
  - Einkorn stands 64–120 cm in central European trials (#144, #145).
- **Reaping.**
  - Short weeds in the stored pulses imply the pulses were pulled by hand (B) (#100). Uprooting cereals is "inconclusive" (#99).
  - Six bronze sickles are known, one 21.8 × 2.1 cm (#101).
  - Thera pulled its crops up by hand until the sickle came, about twenty years before 1987 (#115). It now cuts barley near the ear (#116).
- **What the crops look like** (C).
  - *L. clymenum*: twining, winged stems 30–80 cm, with crimson and lilac flowers from March to May (#156, #157, #159).
  - Lentil grows to 60–75 cm, with white to purple flowers (#148).
  - Faba grows 1.5–2 m, with white flowers spotted black (#152).
  - Flax is pale blue (#165). Coriander has white to pink umbels (#166).
- **Scrub, from Heldreich's 1899 Thera records** (C).
  - Plants recorded on Thera: thyme (*Thymbra capitata*), savory (*Satureja thymbra*), thorny burnet, *Thymelaea hirsuta*, asphodel (*Asphodelus aestivus*) and a sharp rush (#174, #179, #183, #193, #202, #206). Lavender was not recorded (#181).
  - Phrygana rarely stands over 50 cm (#234).
  - Thyme grows 20–50 cm. It has rose-purple flowers from May to September, peaking from 10 to 30 June, and drops its leaves from mid-August to mid-September (#171, #173, #175, #176).
  - Thorny burnet is a grey, spiny hemisphere up to 60 cm (#182, #184).
  - *Thymelaea hirsuta* grows 40–100 cm and has yellowish flowers from October to May (#192, #193).
  - The asphodel is a white candelabra 1–2 m tall, flowering from January to April (#200, #202).
- **Giant reed** (impressions in buildings, A as material).
  - Canes 2–6 m tall and 1–4 cm thick, with leaves 20–80 cm long (#212–#214).
  - Plumes 30–60 cm, from August to October in Greece (#213, #216).
- **Painted plants and what must not appear** (C).
  - Crocus, white lily, sea daffodil, myrtle and ivy grow wild in the Cyclades today (#253, #262, #273, #297, #301).
  - Papyrus is absent from the Greek flora (#277). Date palm is planted only, the Cretan palm is doubtful in the Cyclades, and the dwarf fan palm is not native to Greece (#284).
  - Seasons: crocus flowers from late October to February and is dormant from May to September (#251, #252, #255); white lily flowers in May–June (#258); red lily in July, and it does not grow in the Cyclades (#263); sea daffodil from June to September (#269, #270).
  - Must not appear: prickly pear and agave (16th century AD), tomato, maize, tobacco (Istanbul 1598–1606), eucalyptus, bougainvillea and pistachio (#306–#314). Cypress, pomegranate and carob stay doubtful (#315–#318).

### 3.3 How each was grown or managed, and the default day (late May)

**Trees and vine**

- Olive: the charcoal is mostly cut branches 1.5–5 cm across, read as pruning, coppicing or pollarding, with prunings burnt as fuel (#14 #15); olive and almond were managed from the Middle Bronze Age (#18); olive was also large building timber (#17). Modern practice: pruning once or more every 5 years (#29), 150–180 trees/ha on Crete (#30), vase training (#27). The buried tree near Fira stood in living position next to a second tree 9 m away (#10 #11). Late May: near sea level flowering is over and tiny green fruit are setting; groves above about 300 m are still in flower (modern Crete) (#24).
- Fig: no Bronze Age management evidence. Modern Aegean Turkey: summer and winter pruning, caprifigs hung on the trees 5–20 June (#43). Late May: in full leaf; brebas well grown and near ripe; the first main-crop figs just appearing (#39 #42).
- Grape vine: no Bronze Age training evidence in these sources. Modern: pruned each year to 1–3 m (#46); vineyards ploughed three times with the ard and intercropped with L. clymenum (Thera, 1980s memory) (#51). Late May: in flower (pale green clusters), not yet bearing berries (#48 #50).
- Almond: pruned or pollarded with olive, pear and chestnut (Bottema's reading, B) (#15). Late May: in full leaf with green downy fruit (INFERENCE from #54 #55).
- Deciduous oak: no management evidence. Late May: flowering ending, new leaves turning from grey-green to dark green (Q. pubescens) (#62).
- Kermes oak, junipers, mastic: no management evidence. Late May: evergreen; mastic at the end of its flowering (Iberia) (#86).
- Pine: no management evidence. Late May: end of the pollen season (northern Cyprus) (#68).
- Tamarisk: no management evidence. Late May: could not verify.
- Pear: pruned or pollarded (Bottema's reading, B) (#15). Late May: could not verify.
- Terebinth: no management evidence. Late May: in leaf, flowering over (INFERENCE from #83).

**Field crops**

- **Barley:** sown in October on Thera today after manuring in late August and one ploughing with the ard (#111 #128); grown among vines (#111); pulled up by hand until about 1967, now cut near the ear with a short sickle, stubble grazed (#115 #116 #117); at Akrotiri uprooting of cereals is "inconclusive" and only six bronze sickles are known (#99 #101); straw height at harvest on Thera today 30–40 cm (#109). Sowing method (broadcast or rows) could not verify; Hesiod has seed covered by a man with a mattock (#130). **Late May:** standing ripe or nearly so, losing its green; on Thera today not cut until June; in Attica harvested at the end of May (#111 #137 #147).
- **Wheat and einkorn:** sown in autumn with barley but after it (#132); ears from early March to early April in Attica (#142); harvested after barley (#135 #170); landraces tall and prone to lodging (#140 #141 #145). **Late May:** still ripening or just ripe, behind the barley (#135 #170).
- **Lathyrus clymenum:** sown October to January (traditionally from 21 December), in vineyards one year at a time or in fields; shoot tips picked as greens about 15 March; pulled up by hand in May–June and threshed before all other crops (#100 #112 #113 #118 #121 #159). **Late May:** pods browning, plants being pulled (#112).
- **Pea, L. cicera:** harvested in April on Thera today (#114). **Late May:** field already cleared.
- **Lentil:** ripens first of the crops; pulled up by hand at Akrotiri (B) (#100 #124). **Late May:** harvested or being pulled (INFERENCE from #124).
- **Chickpea, bitter vetch, faba, lupin:** sowing seasons from Theophrastus only (#132 #133); month of harvest on Thera could not verify. **Late May:** could not verify.
- **Flax:** sown in late October; pulled for fibre soon after flowering, before the summer heat (#125). **Late May:** pulled or about to be (INFERENCE).
- **Rotation and fallow:** summer fallow grazed; on Thera today a three-crop rotation replaces fallow (#121 #128). For 1628 BCE: Sarpaki thinks some rotation of pulses and cereals likely but its sequence unknown (B; thesis p. 200).

**Scrub, wet ground and giant reed**

- Thyme: used in Cycladic threshing as a hand rake (#198); Cretan thyme phrygana is a grazing and bee pasture (#175, #238). Late May: in leaf, the first flowers opening; peak bloom is three to five weeks off (#173, #175).
- Savory: no management source. Late May: in flower, pink (#179).
- Lavender: no management source. Late May: in flower with purple bract tufts, if present at all (#180, #181).
- Thorny burnet: today laid on the lee side of threshing floors to trap chaff (#191); dominates burnt ground (#184); spreads by rooting stems into clusters (#190). Late May: just switching to small summer leaves, green fruits ripening to red (#185, #186).
- *Thymelaea*: its leaves in LC I crop stores may come from bushes used to brush the crop at winnowing (#196, #197). Late May: the last flowers of the October–May season (#193).
- Asphodel: no management source. Late May: past flowering, leaves and stalks dying back [snippet only] (#202, #204).
- Rush: grows on wet or saline ground; Akrotiri's wet-ground weeds suggest some fields near water (#207, #208). Late May: in flower, brownish heads (#206).
- Henbane: a weed of waste ground, roads and walls (#210, #211). Late May: in flower (#210).
- Giant reed: canes used in Akrotiri buildings, for example as shelves, to save wood (#212); often planted in Greece (#216). Late May: new canes about 1 m tall rising among last year's full-height canes; no plumes (#217, #216).
- Grasses and annual herbs: grazed by sheep and goats; on Crete the herds leave the lowland from May to October (#238); 1950s Thera kept its scrub for grazing (#237). Late May: mostly dry and yellow to grey-brown, with late flowers after any early-May rain (#226, #228, #230).

- Crocus: saffron is picked by hand from flowers that last only a few days (#242); still gathered from wild plants on Santorini today (#243); *C. sativus* is propagated only by corms (#249) and grown as a field crop only near Kozani today (#254). - Papyrus: Warren argued it could have been cultivated in Bronze Age Greece (#276); absent from the Greek flora today (#277). Late May: nothing to show outdoors.
- Palms: the West House palms are shown pruned (#280); date palms today are only planted in Greece (#284). Late May: evergreen; a date palm would carry small green fruit after its February–April flowering (#290, INFERENCE).
- Myrtle: no cultivation source. Late May: in leaf with flower buds; flowering begins in June (Sicily, #296) or from April (Crete page, #295).
- Ivy: no cultivation source. Late May: evergreen; whether it flowers then is disputed (April–July Crete page #299; August–November Europe #300).
- Reed: no cultivation source. Late May: green, growing canes (INFERENCE from winter die-back #303); plume months could not be verified for the Aegean.

### 3.5 What is still missing

1. **No phenology from Thera or the Cyclades.** Every month comes from somewhere else: Crete, Attica, Aegean Turkey, Cyprus, Italy, Spain, Iran or the United States. Four works would settle most of it, and none opened:
   - Raus 1994 on the central Aegean in winter, including Thera (abstract only);
   - Raus et al. 2019 on Santorini's flora;
   - Arianoutsou-Faraggitaki and Diamantopoulos 1985 on Greek maquis and phrygana month by month;
   - the Flora of Greece web portal.
2. **No ancient size.** No straw height, ear form or fruit size is measured from Akrotiri: olive stone, fig and grape pip sizes were not found in any opened source. Halstead and Jones 1989 (Amorgos reaping height and harvest dates) and work on wild and cultivated olive stones were paywalled or blocked.
3. **Autumn and leaf fall.** No southern Aegean source dates leaf fall or autumn colour for vine, almond, the deciduous oak, terebinth or tamarisk; only fig is dated (#41). The flowering of kermes oak, pear, both junipers and tamarisk could not be verified (tamarisk has only a snippet, #79). Almond bloom has only Spanish data.
4. **Spread and seasonal colour.** No opened source gives the spread of any tree or shrub, or the cushion width of the scrub. Leaf colour by season is missing for most scrub plants and for every crop.
5. **Bulbs and corms, and the painted colours.**
   - The leaf cycles of crocus, white lily and asphodel rest on Iran, one beach on Evvia, the Balearics and a snippet.
   - The painted colours of the palms, myrtle and ivy were not read. Doumas 1992, *The Wall Paintings of Thera*, or a library copy of Morgan 1988 would give them.
6. **Smaller gaps.**
   - Which deciduous oak, evergreen oak, tamarisk and juniper grew on Thera: the charcoal stops at "type".

## Section 4: Assumptions checked

- **T3c-1 (barley ripe in May, golden or being cut in late May; wheat follows): partly confirmed (C).**
  - On Thera today barley is sown in October and harvested in June (#111), with threshing in May and early June (#119).
  - Near Athens it was cut at the end of May (#137).
  - Theophrastus puts the barley harvest in the seventh or eighth month after sowing, with wheat later (#135).
  - Hesiod starts the harvest at the rising of the Pleiades, about 15 May in Boeotia (#127, #129).
  - A folk calendar puts wheat about fifteen days after barley (#170, snippet).
  - So in late May the barley stands ripe and losing its green (#147). On Thera it may still be standing rather than cut. Wheat follows.
- **T3c-2 (olives in flower, grapes small and green, figs unripe in late May): partly contradicted (C).**
  - On Crete the olive's median flowering is 28 April at 45 m, and 9–25 May at 315–624 m (#24). In late May the low groves have finished flowering and are setting fruit; only high groves are in flower.
  - Vines are in flower (15 May–3 June) and have no grapes yet (#48).
  - Figs: the brebas are large and near ripe, while the main crop is only appearing (#39, #42). "Unripe" holds for the main crop.
  - Bronze Age dates may have run a little later (#91).
- **T3c-3 (Bronze Age cereals about 1 m or more): partly confirmed for wheat, not for barley on Thera; no Bronze Age measurement.**
  - Wheat landraces stand 118–159 cm, against 84–104 cm for modern varieties, which carry dwarfing genes (#140, #141). Einkorn stands 64–120 cm (#144, #145).
  - Greek barley landraces reach only 66–81 cm even near Athens (#138). Barley on Thera today reaches 30–40 cm (#109).
  - Small, deformed barley grains in two West House samples hint at a poor or early-cut crop (B) (#98).
  - INFERENCE: 1 m is plausible for wheat on good ground and unlikely for barley on Thera's thin sandy soil.
- **T3c-4 (fig, vine and deciduous oak bare from about December to March; low ground green from the autumn rains to April or May): partly confirmed (C).**
  - Fig is bare from late November or December until mid-March to early April (#38, #41).
  - The vine is dormant until budburst in late March (#48), but no opened source dates its leaf fall.
  - Downy oak leafs out from March (#62). Its leaf fall is not dated, and Valonia oak is semi-evergreen (#63).
  - The low ground greens from November, is greenest from December to February and withers from late April (#222–#225). The crops are sown from October to January and harvested from April to June (#111–#114).
  - The bulbs run the other way: the leaves of crocus and sea daffodil are green from winter to spring and gone in summer (#251, #255, #270).
- **T3c-5 (prickly pear, agave, eucalyptus, citrus and tomato absent): confirmed (C).**
  - Prickly pear and agave are Mexican and reached the Mediterranean in the 16th century AD (#306, #307). Tomato is American (#307). Eucalyptus is Australian, an alien in Greece, and a "recent acquisition" on Santorini (#308, #312).
  - Citrus rests on the earlier check (Section 3.4).
  - Also excluded: maize (first European record found, 1559), tobacco (Istanbul 1598–1606), bougainvillea and pistachio (19th century, snippet) (#309–#311, #313, #314).
- **T3c-6 (by late May most low-altitude grasses and annual herbs are straw-coloured; thyme coming into flower): confirmed (C), with a caveat.**
  - In lowland Crete the summer-dry look prevails by the second and third weeks of May, turning yellow- to grey-brown (#226, #228).
  - Rain in early May sets off late flowering, so not every late May is dry (#230).
  - Thyme begins to flower in May and peaks from 10 to 30 June (#173, #175).
  - On north-facing ground phrygana stays green one to two months longer (Anafi, #236).

## Section 5: Debates

- **Did figs and vines grow on Thera?**
  - Bottema-Mac Gillavry found no fig or vine wood and allowed that the fruit was imported (#36, #45).
  - Fig wood has since been found among the building timbers (#34). One grape pip may be of the wild type (#44).
  - The T3c check notes that Doumas reports vine charcoal; that was not reopened here.
- **Wild or cultivated olive?** The charcoal cannot tell (#15). The Therasia plant was a shrub (#12). The trees near Fira had crowns at least 3 m up (#8).
- **Pine and juniper: local or imported?** Compression wood and branch wood suggest local trees, but building timber may have been brought in (#64, #69). Pine is "scarcely present" in the repairs (#56).
- **Uprooting or the sickle at Akrotiri.**
  - Sarpaki reads the short weeds in the pulse crops as uprooting (B) (#100). She calls uprooting of cereals "inconclusive" (#99).
  - Six bronze sickles and very rare flint sickle elements leave both open (#101).
  - Thera uprooted its crops until about 1967 (#115). Amorgos reaped its cereals low with a sickle (#169, snippet).
- **Which wheat.** The LC IA kitchen held one free-threshing grain, *Triticum* cf. *aestivo-compactum* (#96). The West House held einkorn as a crop and traces of emmer (#94, #95). The island durum landraces of today (#140) do not show what grew in 1628.
- **Einkorn's height.** One central European study gives a mean of 73 cm in its text and 85 cm in its abstract (#144); another gives 106.7 cm (#145).
- **The phase of the flax and lupin.** Both come only from Delta room Δ1a, and the thesis labels sample 40 "M.B.A." (#104, #106). If that holds, they predate the last town.
- **When thorny burnet flowers.** From mid-February to early March in lowland Crete (#184); April–June in Halácsy (#183); March–May on a web flora (#189). Flora months are often second-hand (#240).
- **Which asphodel.** *A. aestivus*, Halácsy's *A. microcarpus*, is the plant recorded on Thera. *A. ramosus* in the narrow sense reaches only south-west Greece (#200–#202).
- **Which reed.** The impressions are *Arundo donax* (#212). The painted reeds (#302) are described here with the common reed *Phragmites* as a modern stand-in (#303–#305); no opened source names their species. No opened source compares the impressions with the slimmer *A. plinii*.
- **Lavender on Thera.** It is named in the kitchen remains (T3 #7) but was not recorded on Thera in 1899 (#181). Whether it grew there or was brought in is open.
- **Which crocus.** The painted crocus may be *C. sativus* or the wild *C. cartwrightianus* (#246). The crocus on Santorini today is *C. laevigatus*, which flowers white or lilac in winter (#248, #252).
- **Which lily, and when the red one flowers.**
  - The painted red lilies have the white lily's buds and leaves (#260).
  - Sources disagree on *L. chalcedonicum*'s month: "late spring" (#257), June–July (#259), or "generally July" (#263).
- **Sea daffodil or papyrus in the House of the Ladies.** Sea daffodil, with blue heads and seven stamens (#267, #268). Papyrus, by Doumas's caption (#274).
- **The sea daffodil's leaves at flowering.** They are still present in June on Evvia (#270, #271) but already dry by August in the Balearics (#272).
- **When ivy flowers.** April–July on a Cretan web flora (#299); August–November in a European database (#300). Neither is an Aegean field record.
- **Which palm.** Date palm or *Phoenix theophrasti* (#281). Neither is attested by wood. The dwarf fan palm grows on Santorini today (#282) but is not native to Greece (#284).

## Section 6: Game hooks (suggestions only, not evidence)

- **One curve for the ground.** Drive the colour of the grass and herb layer from a single curve keyed to the date:
  - green from November to February;
  - flecked yellow from late March to April;
  - straw from mid-May;
  - grey-brown from July to October.

  Every plant table then hangs off that curve. A "wet May" toggle could hold some green and late flowers into the default day (#230).
- **Late May as a working landscape.** A patchwork of:
  - ripe barley standing pale gold;
  - *L. clymenum* plots being pulled, the plants heaped brown;
  - bare plots where pea was lifted in April;
  - threshing floors with animals treading;
  - olive groves past flower;
  - vines in pale bloom;
  - figs in heavy leaf.
- **Low barley on Thera.** Use the 30–40 cm of modern Thera for barley in the town's fields. Use taller wheat (about 1–1.3 m) only on the best ground. Record both heights as liberties, since no Bronze Age figure exists.
- **Pruned olives.** Draw olives low and cut back (pollard or coppice shapes), not as 15 m trees: the charcoal is prunings and the buried trees' crowns started 1–3 m up. Record the shape as a liberty with its B source.
- **Paintings only.** Keep papyrus, palms, red lilies and the saffron crocus inside paintings and on pots. Outdoors, the crocus of the island is the small winter-flowering *C. laevigatus*, not a purple autumn carpet.
- **Wind-shaped juniper** on sea cliffs is sourced as a habit (#72). Bending other trees by the wind is not sourced and would be a liberty.
