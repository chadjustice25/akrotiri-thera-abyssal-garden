## How grades and dates are applied in this lane (read first)
- **A:** found at Akrotiri in the Late Cycladic I (LC I) town, or a laboratory analysis of such material (lead isotopes, X-ray diffraction of the lead weights, fish bone).
- **B:** shown in Theran art (the West House paintings are the main case: "shown is not present"); found at a contemporary Aegean site and carried over; or found at Akrotiri but its reading is debated.
- **C:** later evidence (Uluburun, Kommos Building P, Hesiod), analogy, modern reconstruction (the Heidelberg models, the *Minoa* replica, sailing-time models), or hypothesis.
- **Phase and date:** Akrotiri's last phase is LC I = Late Minoan IA (LM IA), ended by the eruption. Following X1 (#26, #27): **Aegean high** LM IA c. 1700/1675–1625/1600 BCE; **Aegean low** LM IA c. 1600–1480 BCE. "Eruption" in this column means the end of LC I under either chronology. Dates a source gives in its own terms are repeated as the source gives them, with its chronology if it names one.
- **Words.** "Ikria" (stern cabins) is a later Greek word used by modern scholars; no Bronze Age word for any part of a ship is known, and Linear A is undeciphered. The same holds for "Keftiu" (an Egyptian word, not a Minoan one).
- **INFERENCE** marks this agent's own reasoning or arithmetic. **[verify]** marks a claim that rests on one source, or on a later author's report of an older work.

## Section 1: Summary

1. **No Bronze Age Aegean hull has been found; every ship fact comes from pictures.** The West House frieze is the richest picture, so every ship in the world is at most grade B, and every dimension is grade C (#33, #1).
2. **The fleet:** 14 craft, 7 of them large (Strasser), or 6 processional ships plus one rowed and one sailing ship (Wachsmann). The second scene shows undecorated ships of the same shape beached after a fight (#1–#3).
3. **Length of the large ships is open: 9.7–23.6 m (Heidelberg, from 0.9 m paddle spacing) or up to 33.75 m (Marinatos).** The beam is about 1/5 to 1/6.5 of the length in Gillmer's estimate, 5.2–6.75 m at 33.75 m (#4–#7).
4. **The large ships are shown paddled, with sails lowered and some masts unstepped.** Wachsmann reads paddling as an archaic, ceremonial mode, not the norm. One ship sails under a square sail with yard and boom (four lifts), rigged like Egyptian ships (#10–#13).
5. **Hull and fittings:** the hull curves up more at the stern than at the bow. The high bow has a decorated pole with a bird or butterfly figurehead. Each large ship has a stern cabin and a detachable horizontal stern projection lashed with two lines. The helmsman stands at the stern with a steering oar (#14–#17, #20).
6. **Harbours: ships are beached or anchored, not moored at quays.** The arrival town has three boats pulled up on a beach and two at anchor. Bronze Age harbours used open beaches, peninsulas and islets. Akrotiri's own harbour is unfound; one proposal puts it just west of the town (#23, #53, #57; T1 #36–#37).
7. **Season and speed (grade C):** summer sailing, with ships hauled ashore in winter. A day's sail is up to 100–150 km. Square-sail speeds are 3–6 knots. Akrotiri to north Crete is ≈115 km in a straight line (INF), a long day's sail southbound in summer (#56, #59–#62).
8. **Where the fleet goes is unsettled:** Akrotiri itself, two caldera towns, or a generic scene (#27–#29).
9. **Trade partners at the moment (LC I):** Crete is foremost (stone vases, imported cups). Naxos supplied many pots, and the mainland link is strong. Cyprus is attested by one White Slip I bowl, and the Levant by Canaanite jars of debated origin (#66–#71).
10. **Metals:** lead at LC I Akrotiri came almost entirely from Laurion (Lavrion), by isotope analysis. Copper provenance for Akrotiri itself could not be verified. Cypriot copper on Crete is LM IB, after the eruption (#73–#76).
11. **Weights:** 102 lead disc weights were found, against ≈36 from all Crete. The West House set has 26 discs, from 11 g to 5.5 kg, and a balance with bronze pans was found stored in a pithos. The Minoan unit is 62–65.5 g, but a c. 180 g system and possible Syrian and Egyptian units also occur (#78–#84).
12. **Fishing:** the fish bones are of very small shallow-water fish. A plant-fibre net and a trammel net are reported from Akrotiri. The painted catch is dolphinfish and little tunny (grade B, shown) (#47–#52).

## Section 4: Assumptions checked

- **T5-1** (ceremonial longships moved mainly by paddlers, with mast and sail): **partly confirmed.** The processional ships are shown paddled, with masts and sails lowered or masts unstepped (#10, #11, #12); one ship sails and one is rowed (#1). Whether the scene is a ceremony is debated (#28–#31), and Wachsmann reads the paddling as an archaic, non-normal mode, not the ships' usual drive (#10, #30).
- **T5-2** (largest ships roughly 20–35 m, 20–40 paddlers or rowers): **partly confirmed for length, not verified for crew.** Estimates run 9.7–23.6 m (Heidelberg, #4) to 33.75 m (Marinatos, #6). No opened scholarly source gave a paddler count per ship; the *Minoa* replica (15 m) had 22–24 rowers (#43, #44), and a popular source gives 15 oars per side (#36, C).
- **T5-3** (the fleet arrives at Akrotiri): **could not verify; debated.** Some identify the arrival town as Akrotiri (#27); others place both towns inside the caldera (#28, #29) or treat the scene as generic (#27). Lane T1b covers the landscape.
- **T5-4** (Crete the main partner; links to mainland, Dodecanese, Cyprus, Levant): **partly confirmed.** Crete dominates imported stone vases and imported cups (#66, #69), but Naxos was a large source of pots (#67), and the mainland link is strong (#65, #66). The Dodecanese link is attested for the MC period (#68). Cyprus rests on one White Slip I bowl (#70); the Levant on Canaanite jars of debated origin (#71).
- **T5-5** (copper mainly from Lavrion and Cyprus): **partly contradicted / could not verify.** Lead at LC I Akrotiri was virtually all Laurion (#73), and Cretan copper was largely Laurion from MM I (#75). No copper provenance study of Akrotiri objects could be opened (Stos-Gale & Gale 1990 was not reachable). The Cypriot (Apliki) copper on Crete is LM IB, after the eruption (#76), so Cyprus as a copper source for Akrotiri is unsupported.
- **T5-6** (Akrotiri used the Minoan weight standard): **partly confirmed.** The Minoan unit (c. 60–65.5 g) fits Akrotiri (#79, #83), but Petruso put many weights in a non-Minoan c. 180 g system (#79), and possible Syrian and Egyptian units are present (#84).
- **T5-7** (Thera to Crete in about a day): **partly confirmed (by inference).** The straight line is ≈115 km (INF), inside the 100–150 km day's-sail upper limit (#59); at 3–6 knots that is ≈10–21 hours under way (INF). Giesecke thought a rowed Theran ship could do it in a day (#63, C). Wind direction matters greatly: southbound is easier in summer (#61, #62, by analogy).

## Section 5: Debates

1. **What the frieze shows.**
   - A real expedition, raid or joint venture: Warren judges raiding and Mycenaean–Minoan cooperation equally plausible (#21, #31). Shaw lists "possible Bronze Age relations with Libya" among the questions the frieze has raised (Shaw 1990, source 3).
   - Festival or ceremony using archaic ships or archaic handling: Casson, followed by Wachsmann (#30); L. M. Brown's festival voyage (as cited, #31).
   - A local procession inside the caldera: Friedrich & Sørensen (#28); Strasser (#29).
2. **Paddling versus rowing versus sail.** Wachsmann: paddling is depicted but not normal; the sail was the working drive (#10, #11). Heidelberg: these ships could do all three, the sail being preferred, paddles and oars needed to enter bays (#19). Shaw: paddling may reflect a head wind (#24).
3. **Ship length.** Heidelberg 9.7–23.6 m from oar spacing (#4, #5) against Marinatos's 33.75 m (#6). The gap is the difference between counting depicted paddlers at a real spacing and taking the painting's proportions; Wachsmann's 2019 *Arts* paper on reading watercraft images is relevant but could not be opened.
4. **Is the arrival town Akrotiri?** Yes (N. Marinatos, Doumas, Morgan as cited); generic type-scene (Shaw citing Groenewegen-Frankfort, Warren) (#27); caldera sites Ballos/Raos (#28).
5. **Ship-sheds.** M.C. Shaw reads the tall open-bayed building as ship-sheds; Marinatos read it as a dairy (#25). The excavated parallels (Kommos P, Nirou Khani) are later or of uncertain function (#54).
6. **The Akrotiri harbour.** West of the site between Mavro Rachidi and Mesovouna (Pichler, Doumas) versus the caldera as a harbour (Heiken et al.), which Shaw rejects (#57). T1 #36–#37 adds that the harbour has not been found.
7. **Weight standards.** A single Minoan unit (c. 60–65.5 g) versus several systems at once, including c. 180 g, Syrian and Egyptian units (#79, #83, #84). Many identifications of stone objects as weights are themselves uncertain (#84).
8. **Dating the imports.** The White Slip I bowl and the Canaanite jars are central to the eruption-date debate; their own dates are disputed (#70, #71; see X1).
9. **Room numbering.** Wachsmann and most sources put the frieze in Room 5; Shaw 1990 writes "Room 4" for the north-wall scene (#25). This report follows Room 5.

## Section 6: Game hooks (suggestions only, not evidence)

- *Suggestion:* Build three hull classes from the frieze: the decorated processional ship (stern cabin, bow pole with a bird or butterfly figurehead, lashed stern projection, mast lowered), the working ship with sail set (yard and boom, four lifts), and small open boats. Keep the large-ship length a parameter between ≈10 and ≈34 m and record the chosen value as a liberty.
- *Suggestion:* Default the harbour scene to boats pulled up stern-first on a sandy beach and a few at anchor, as the frieze shows, rather than a quay (#23, #53).
- *Suggestion:* In winter, haul boats ashore and empty the sea of sails; in summer, let traffic arrive mostly from the north and leave southward (#56, #62).
- *Suggestion:* Make a merchant's storeroom with a disc-weight set and bronze balance pans in a pithos (#81, #82), and a fish-drying or processing corner with small shallow-water fish (#47, #49).
- *Suggestion:* Imports on shelves should be mostly Cretan and Naxian pots and Cretan stone vases, with at most one rare Cypriot bowl and a Canaanite jar shown as uncertain (#66, #67, #69–#71).
- *Suggestion:* A viewer's question "Where are they sailing?" can be answered by showing the competing readings (#28–#31), not one.
