## How this lane reads the grades and dates (read first)
- **A:** seen in the excavated LC I town at Akrotiri (a wall, a floor, a measured room, a find in place), or a laboratory analysis of its material (charcoal). A measurement taken by the excavators of something that stood in the last town is A.
- **B:** the excavators' or specialists' interpretation of A-grade remains where the reading is debated (what a room was used for, whether a building was "public"); evidence from Crete carried over; or something shown in Theran wall painting.
- **C:** an estimate (town size, population), a modern drawn or digital reconstruction, an analogy, or a hypothesis.
- **Most of what this lane reports is the excavators' own description of the excavated buildings.** That makes it A-grade for the remains, but it rests on a few authors (Doumas, Palyvou, Michailidou). Palyvou's monograph (2005) could not be opened in a licensed form; several of its numbers are reported here through Michailidou (2013), who quotes it with page numbers. Those rows say so.
- **Dates.** Phase keys used in the "Phase and date" column, following X1 (`docs/research/claude/X1_Claude.md`, not yet verified):
  - **K1 = final LC I (Late Cycladic I = Late Minoan IA), the town as buried.** High chronology: LC I 1700/1675–1625/1600 BCE (X1#26); burial c. 1625 BCE (X1#28), Manning's most likely eruption date c. 1611 BCE (X1#62). Low chronology: LM IA 1600–1480 BCE (X1#27); burial c. 1550/1540 BCE (X1#28). Distance from the eruption: 0 (it is the abandonment horizon). Doumas (2016) gives the heyday as c. 1650–1615 BC and the eruption "around 1615 BC" (his own dates, close to the high chronology). Michailidou (2013) gives "around 1600 B.C."; Fotou and Michailidou (2006) write "16th–15th century B.C." (an older low framing).
  - **K2 = the LC I rebuilding after the earthquake of the mid-17th century BC (Doumas).** High c. 1650 BCE, low c. 1560 BCE (X1#28). Doumas and Mavromati put it about 50 years before the eruption; on Rutter's low dates the gap is shorter (c. 10–25 years). The interval is disputed (X1 Section 4).
  - **K3 = Middle Cycladic town**, 2000–1650 BC in Doumas (2016); between c. 350 and c. 50 years before the eruption on his dates. **K4 = Early Cycladic**, 3rd millennium BC. **K5 = modern** (a plan, a database, a reconstruction).
- **Names.** Every building, street and square name (West House, Xeste 3, Telchines Street, Triangle Square and the rest) is a modern excavation label given by Marinatos or Doumas. "Xeste" (dressed-stone building) is Marinatos's modern Greek term. No ancient name is known for any building, street or for the town. The Linear A signs on one jug are transliterated by the excavator; their meaning is unknown.

## Section 1: Summary
- **What is excavated is small and central.** About 1–1.2 ha has been dug, holding about 35 houses; the whole town is estimated at 10 ha (Palyvou, about 2,000 people) to 20 ha (Doumas, tentative). Its edges are unknown. (#1–#5, #103)
- **Houses are compact, dense and two to three storeys high.** A typical house covers 96–187 m², the big ones 300–370 m² (Xeste 3: 283 m², Xeste 4: about 367 m², House of the Ladies: 184 m²). No courtyards; each house has one entrance, usually at a corner, with a window beside it. (#6, #8, #19, #24, #44, #102, #111)
- **The streets were raised by earthquake debris.** After the mid-LC I earthquake the rubble was spread in streets and squares, so old ground floors became semi-basements about 1 m below the street, reached by a few steps down from the antechamber; a stone stair leads up. (#11, #14, #56, #80)
- **Streets: paved, drained, narrow.** The rebuilt streets were paved with large flagstones on gradients, over a sewer that house latrines drained into; one alley is 1.10 m wide. The widths of the main streets and squares could not be found. (#10–#13, #36, #74)
- **Walls: rubble and earth in a timber frame, dressed with tuff.** Outer walls at the West House openings are 0.60–0.75 m thick [verify]; horizontal timbers run at three levels at a corner; doors and windows are framed in off-white tuff ashlar, with tuff string courses and hard-stone quoins. Only the "xestai" have full ashlar façades. (#39–#43, #65–#69, #20, #24)
- **Floors and roofs are heavy earth on timber.** Unworked trunks (one 0.16–0.18 m thick), a layer of branches, 10–15 cm (or about 20 cm) of rammed earth and thin clay skims; flat roof terraces drain through parapet spouts, one sloping 25% to its spout. Timbers were mostly olive, pine, oak and juniper. (#70–#76)
- **Ground floors stored and processed; upper floors lived, worked and were painted.** Pithoi rooms, mills and kitchens below; painted rooms, weaving rooms (one with 400+ loom weights) and latrines above, with exceptions (Xeste 3, Delta 2). (#31–#37, #46, #84, #33, #91)
- **Light came through pier-and-window walls.** The West House's best room (about 4 × 4 m) has two outer walls of four openings each, 3.90 and 4.00 m long; its paintings assume an eye level of about 1.50 m. (#34, #35, #42)
- **Named buildings with known plans:** Xeste 3 (three storeys, 15 ground-floor rooms, lustral basin, painted halls), Xeste 4 (white tuff ashlar, painted grand staircase), West House, House of the Ladies (light-well), the four houses of Complex Delta, Complex Beta, Building Gamma and the Pithoi Storeroom. (#19–#63)
- **No palace has been found.** The excavator calls Xeste 3 and Xeste 4 public buildings and only guesses that Xeste 4 served the town's administration. (#18, #26, #64)
- **The last scene is a town under repair, then emptied.** Debris heaped in the streets up to door lintels, dangerous walls demolished, upper doors walled up, beds and pots carried outside ready to go, doors shut, valuables gone. (#93–#99)
- **Missing numbers:** storey heights, main-street widths, furniture sizes, exterior plaster colour and any "Minoan foot". Palyvou's 1999, 2005 and 2019 books are where they will be; none could be opened. Until then they are liberties. (Section 3)

## Section 4: Assumptions checked

- **T2-1 (two or three storeys, flat roofs):** confirmed, with nuance. Two to three storeys is the norm and Xeste 4 may have had more in its east wing (#19, #24, #44, #86, #110). Roofs were flat earth terraces with clay skims, parapets and projecting terracotta spouts, but one preserved roof slopes 25% toward its spout, so "flat" means a drained terrace, not level (#70–#73).
- **T2-2 (rubble with clay mortar, timber-framed, ashlar on important buildings):** partly confirmed. Walls are fieldstone and earth with timber frames (#65, #67, #43). Ashlar is more widespread than the assumption says: most buildings have off-white tuff ashlar frames round doors and windows, string courses and hard-stone quoins (#68, #69); full ashlar façades are confined to the "xestai", and in Xeste 3 only to its east part (#20, #24). Partitions also used mud brick and pisé (#23, #62). "Clay mortar" as such is given only as "earth" in the sources opened.
- **T2-3 (paved streets, covered drain beneath, joined to house latrines):** partly confirmed. The rebuilt streets were paved with relatively large flagstones over a wastewater system that followed their gradients, and house sanitary installations were connected to it (#11–#13, #36, #74). The drain is "covered" in Shaw's account (#74). But at least one side street is described as cobbled (#60), streets had been raised with debris, and no source opened says every street was paved.
- **T2-4 (about 1 ha excavated of a town of about 20 ha):** partly confirmed. About 1–1.2 ha is excavated (#1, #2, #4, #103). The 20 ha figure is Doumas's tentative estimate (#3); Palyvou's more modest estimate is 10 ha with about 2,000 people (#2).
- **T2-5 (ground floors storage and work; painted rooms mostly upstairs):** confirmed, with exceptions. Ground floors, often semi-basements after the earthquake, held storage, processing and food preparation (#14, #31, #46, #61); paintings decorate upper floors as a rule (#84). Exceptions: Xeste 3's painted ground-floor Room 4 and lustral basin (#21, #22) and the Spring Fresco in ground-floor Delta 2 (#51). Upper floors also held workshops (weaving: #33, #91).
- **T2-6 (no single palace):** partly confirmed. No palace has been found in the excavated part, and the excavator sees two "public" buildings, Xeste 3 and Xeste 4, with Xeste 4 tentatively linked to administration (#18, #26). Doumas also infers a centralised management of public works from the Middle Cycladic streets and drains (#15). No source opened states in so many words that Akrotiri had no palace, and 90% of the town is unexcavated.
- **T2-7 (laid out with a "Minoan foot" of about 30 cm):** could not verify. The one source opened says discrete units were most likely used but gives no length (#105).

## Section 5: Debates

1. **How big was the town, and how many lived there?** Doumas tentatively proposed about 200,000 m² (20 ha); Palyvou in 1986 thought this unusually large for an Aegean Late Bronze Age site (#3), and her own estimate (2005) is about 100,000 m² and about 2,000 people, called the most modest yet (#2). Neither figure rests on excavation of the town's edges, which are unknown.
2. **"Public" buildings and the palace question.** Doumas classifies Xeste 3 and Xeste 4 as public edifices, Xeste 3 for rites of passage and Xeste 4 possibly for administration (#18, #21, #26). Fotou and Michailidou caution that the dwelling is the only Neopalatial building type we can be sure of, and read Xeste 3 as a dwelling combined with a public, ritual function (#64). There is no palace in the excavated area, but the question of where authority sat is open.
3. **Who used the "special function" ground floors, and how?** Marinatos read the Pithoi Storeroom as a distribution centre paying workers in kind (by analogy with Linear B palaces); a later reading makes it a family-run food shop; Delta 16, with 400 sorted, unused vessels, is read as a shop with its window as counter (#57–#60). All rest on find patterns, not on texts.
4. **How thick were floors and roofs?** Doumas gives a rammed-earth layer of about 10–15 cm (#70); Palyvou (as quoted by Michailidou) about 0.20 of packed soil plus clay skims (#71). The quoted unit ("o,2o cm") is garbled, so the original must be checked.
5. **How many storeys had the West House?** Doumas writes "at least two-storeyed", with a second storey inferred from debris (#30, #38); Palyvou (2020 interview) and the Ephorate call it three-storey (#102, #110). The difference is about a partial top floor (a shelter and one room).
6. **The last weeks.** The excavators describe a population that came back after the final earthquake, demolished dangerous walls, walled up doors, carried beds and pots outside, then left with their valuables and shut their doors before the eruption (#93–#95). The Ephorate's public summary says the eruption came a few dozen days after the earthquake, describes a temporary camp, and suggests people had no chance to leave the island (#101). The interval is X1's and the event lanes' question; this lane records only what was seen.
7. **Where were the kitchens?** No fixed hearth was found in the three fully excavated buildings; hearth-like fixtures in Alpha 1 and Delta 1a show little burning; the two soot-blackened rooms in pillar shafts 1B and 65N are the clearest "kitchens", and Birtacha leaves open how many people each served (#79–#83). Portable tripod ovens and cooking pots (#90) may have done much of the cooking.
8. **Is the "arrival town" in the West House miniature frieze Akrotiri?** Michailidou allows that the town in the frieze "may possibly" be Akrotiri; Trinks and colleagues test the idea that its hilltop buildings stood on Kokkino Vouno, where radar found Bronze Age walls (#104). The frieze is grade B at most for Thera's appearance (shown is not present).

## Section 6: Game hooks (suggestions, not evidence)

- **Build the semi-basement.** Streets about 1 m higher than the old ground floors (#11, #56, #80) give every house a few steps down from the antechamber into dim storerooms and a stone stair up. This is the strongest single cue for "Akrotiri" rather than a generic Bronze Age town.
- **One house type, many variations.** A procedural rule set: footprint 96–187 m² (#6), one entrance at a corner with a window beside it (#14, #111), stair from the antechamber, storerooms below, a large central-column room and a pier-and-window room above (#32–#35), latrine draining to the street (#36), flat drained roof with parapet and spouts (#72, #73). Two or three "xeste" landmarks in white tuff ashlar break the pattern (#20, #24).
- **Colour from materials, not paint.** Off-white tuff frames and string courses on grey-brown rubble walls, hard-stone quoins, a red-painted ignimbrite stair in the grand buildings (#28, #65, #68, #69). Exterior plaster colour is unknown: leave rubble visible or mark plaster as a liberty.
- **A town under repair.** Debris heaps in the streets up to door lintels (#96), propped and leaning walls, walled-up upper doors, beds and pots stacked outside ready to go, unfinished clearing, doors shut (#93–#95). This is the moment the project depicts; it is quieter and stranger than a bustling market.
- **Interior props from casts and finds.** Pithoi rows along walls, tripod cooking pots and portable ovens (#90), large table lamps and small hand lamps (#88), loom weights in hundreds where the light is best (#33), a carved tripod table and a balustraded armrest (#87), a monolithic stone pithos in a square (#53). Furniture sizes are unknown and need liberties.
- **Light.** The paintings were composed for an eye at about 1.50 m (#35) and lit by big south and west pier-and-window walls; ground floors were dim (#31). A day–night cycle in Room 5 would show what the building was designed to do.
