## How to read the dates and grades (read first)
- **Phase labels used in the Claims table:**
  - **LC I-F**: the final occupation and destruction level of Akrotiri (Late Cycladic I = late Late Minoan IA), sealed by the eruption. It lies 0 years from the eruption. Its absolute date is open. Under the **Aegean high** (radiocarbon) chronology the burial is c. 1625 BCE; under the **Aegean low** (Egyptian-linked) chronology it is c. 1550/1540 BCE (X1#28). See `docs/research/claude/X1_Claude.md` for the full debate.
  - **LC I-W**: a wall painting on the walls when the town was abandoned. It was painted during LC I, at an unknown time before the eruption (0 to a few decades, INFERENCE from X1#29 on the interval between the earthquake and the eruption).
  - **LC I**: Late Cycladic I, with no precise level given by the source.
  - Evidence from **Crete** is given as Late Minoan (LM) or Middle Minoan (MM) phases. Egyptian dates follow the source. Where a date falls outside the 1800–1500 BCE window, the distance is stated and the claim is grade C.
- **Grades** follow `docs/HISTORICAL_STANDARD.md` §3. A = found on Thera in an LC I context, or a lab analysis of such material. B = shown in Theran art (shown is not present), or found elsewhere in the Aegean at the time, or found on Thera but its meaning is debated. C = later evidence, analogy, modern reconstruction or hypothesis. For people this lane adds one rule: **any reading of what a painted scene means (rite, age, status, illness) is at best B, and a single scholar's new hypothesis is C.**
- **OCR note.** In the extracted text of Michailidou 2000–2001 (Minos 35–36), the Greek capital Delta (Δ) of the excavation's room labels was read as "A". "A 18a" in that text is room Δ18a of Building Complex Delta. The excavation's caption tradition and the article's own phrase "complex Delta" support this (INFERENCE; verify on the printed page).
- **Names and words.** No Bronze Age name for Thera, Akrotiri or any person on the island is known from the sources opened. Linear A is undeciphered (#70). Building names (West House, Xeste 3, House of the Ladies, House of the Benches, Complex Delta) are modern excavation labels. Figure names ("Necklace Swinger", "Wounded Woman", "Veiled Girl", "Five Mature Ladies", "Young Priestess", "Boxing Boys") are modern nicknames.

## Section 1: Summary
1. **The town's own remains say a lot about daily life, and nothing about bodies.** No human skeletons and few portable valuables were found. The people seem to have left before the eruption (#24). Stature and health therefore cannot come from Akrotiri. No opened source gave Aegean Bronze Age stature figures (Section 4, T4 gaps).
2. **Food is well evidenced (grade A).** The staples were barley, einkorn and other wheat, lentil, pea and grass pea (*Lathyrus clymenum*), with fig, grape and olive. Food was stored partly processed: split pulses, cracked barley and flour (#28–#31). Pests in stored pulses point to a summer eruption, after the harvest (#32).
3. **Meat came mostly from domestic animals.** A kitchen deposit held at least 8 sheep and goats, pigs and 2 cattle. Fish and shellfish were a smaller share of animal protein (#47–#49). Fish was eaten small and often: sea breams and picarels, a fish paste, split and salted dentex, and stored red porgy. A tuna steak was left cooking in a pan on the hearth (#33–#38).
4. **A real kitchen can be built to measure.** Pillar Shaft 65N was a sooty room of 1.30 × 1.70 m. It held a two-storey built hearth 0.80 × 0.70 × 1.08 m with its working top about 1 m above the floor, a second hearth in a blocked door, and a flue socket. Eight andesite querns lay on the floor, with olive stones and dung burned as fuel (#42–#46). The three fully excavated houses have no fixed hearth (#39).
5. **Cloth survives.** The finds include fine linen with 20–22 threads per cm, hemmed and embroidered, with knotted fringes and tassels. They also include coarser linen, a bag and a net, and wool yarn (#7–#10). Discoid clay loom weights of 130–290 g fit fine-thread weaving on a warp-weighted loom (#11–#13). True murex purple was found at Akrotiri (#15).
6. **Painted dress (grade B):** women wear flounced skirts and short-sleeved open jackets that bare the breasts, very large earrings, and mantles. Saffron-yellow and red dyed garments carry crocus decoration (#1–#6). The men shown are often nude (fishermen, youths), or wear a white loincloth (#76). Kilts at Thera were not confirmed by an opened source.
7. **Hair marks age (grade B, widely accepted).** A blue scalp means a shaved head. Children and youths of both sexes keep a few long locks, and the number and length of the locks change with age (#16–#19).
8. **Skin colour is a convention:** white for females and red-brown for males, with a yellowish boy as an exception (#20–#21). Specialist work on what the convention means could not be opened (Section 5).
9. **Writing was local and economic.** Two or three unfired Linear A tablets came from storeroom Δ18a in Complex Delta, one of them recording counted textiles. There is also an ostrakon with signs and the numeral forty, a few inscribed pots, and flat-based sealings with Cretan motifs (#58–#68). The Delta houses look like merchants' houses, with weights up to a "half-talent" (#54, #65).
10. **Belief:** Xeste 3 is a public building with a sunken lustral basin and paintings of girls gathering crocus for a seated goddess flanked by a monkey and a griffin. It also shows blood-dripping horns of consecration and male youths' scenes (#71–#77). Specialists agree it shows some rite of passage or initiation, but disagree on which (#79–#82). Physical cult finds include a gold ibex beside a pile of horns, bronze double axes, rhyta (including two bull rhyta), an offering table, and a bronze worshipper figurine (#84–#90).
11. **Sound (mostly grade B or C):** monkeys play lyres in Xeste 3 paintings (shown, not present). Seven-string lyres, paired pipes, sistra, cymbals and probably triton-shell trumpets are known in the Aegean. No drum is depicted or found (#94–#102). Grade-A everyday sounds include querns, looms, sheep, goats, pigs, cattle, dogs and mice (#45, #47, #103).
12. **Names:** Egyptian "Keftiu" and Mesopotamian/Levantine "Kaptara/Caphtor" are generally taken as Crete, not Thera. Most texts with Keftiu words are 14th century BCE (outside the window). Mari's Kaptara goods fall inside it (#104–#109). No Bronze Age name for Thera was found (Section 4, T4-5).

## Section 4: Assumptions checked

- **T4-1 (women: flounced skirts and open short-sleeved bodices; men: loincloths or short kilts): partly confirmed.** The women's dress is confirmed in the paintings (#1, #4, #5), and actual linen with hems, embroidery and fringes supports its realism (#7, #8). Mature women also wear mantles (#5). For men, the opened sources show nudity (fishermen, youths, #76) and a white loincloth on an older man (#76). Kilts at Thera **could not verify** from an opened source; Rehak 1996 on Aegean breechcloths and kilts was not opened.
- **T4-2 (partly shaved heads with long locks mark children and youths at different ages): confirmed as the accepted reading (grade B).** Blue scalps are read as shaved heads, and the number and length of locks track age (#16–#19). It applies to both sexes (#17). The exact age stages are interpretation, not record.
- **T4-3 (red-brown men and white women is a convention, not real skin colour): partly confirmed.** The opened sources call it a convention (#20), and it has exceptions, such as a yellowish boy (#21). Specialist work on what it meant (Newman 2017 and others) **could not be opened**, so its meaning is unsettled here.
- **T4-4 (the Linear A documents from Akrotiri are few and administrative): partly confirmed.** They are few: two or three tablets, one ostrakon, a handful of inscribed pots and one ink inscription (#58–#62, #68). The tablets and ostrakon are economic records (#60, #62). But some inscriptions are on pots or a ritual-looking triton (#61, #68), the sealings carry no script (#64), and whether the tablets form an "archive" is doubted (#59).
- **T4-5 (no Bronze Age name for Thera is known): could not verify from a specialist source.** No opened source gives a Bronze Age name, and none of the Aegean place names read in Amenhotep III's list is Thera (#105, INFERENCE). A specialist statement that the name is unknown was not found among opened sources. Kalliste and Thera are later Greek names (Herodotus 4.147); the Perseus page was opened but the chapter text was not read, so this is listed under sources not opened.
- **T4-6 (cereals and pulses were staples, with fish and shellfish important): partly confirmed.** Cereals and pulses: confirmed, grade A (#28–#30, #41). Fish and shellfish: present and processed in many ways (#33–#38), but the zooarchaeologist judges their share of animal protein smaller than that of domestic mammals (#48).
- **T4-7 (the Xeste 3 saffron gathering shows a female coming-of-age rite): partly confirmed; debated.** Most specialists accept some rite of passage or initiation (#80), but readings range across puberty, marriage, menarche, healing, myth and economic activity (#79, #81). Mature women also gather saffron (#78), and the building has a male cycle too (#76).

## Section 5: Debates

**1. What the Xeste 3 paintings show.**
- *Female initiation or rite of passage:* the dominant reading since Marinatos (1984) and Davis (1986). The paintings are read as girls' passage from childhood to nubility, perhaps at a spring festival (Cameron, Niemeier, as summarised by Fournier) (#80; https://www.gerflint.fr/Base/Europe11/fournier.pdf).
- *Two cycles, male and female:* Davis (2000), Doumas (purification and dressing), Karageorghis (hair-shaving rite) and M. Shaw (circumcision). No cutting tools have been found to support these readings (#76, #77).
- *Marriage or defloration:* Marinatos (1993), Koehl (2001), Chapin (#79; Gallou 2018).
- *Healing:* a saffron healing goddess (Ferrence and Bendersky 2004, #81). Gallou adds the treatment of primary amenorrhoea (2018, #79). Both are hypotheses.
- *Women's roles across life rather than a change of status:* mature women also gather saffron (Vlachopoulos 2008 via Fournier, #78). Rehak (2007) proposes priestesses teaching girls (#82).
- *Critical voice not opened:* Valentinová (Heidelberg, Propylaeum) questions the rite-of-passage reading. Her article was blocked, so her argument is known only from a search summary.

**2. The age of the "Five Mature Ladies".** Chapin (2002) read them as fertile women. Younger (2009, 2016) reads them as past menopause, from their bulky bodies, red-streaked eyes and snoods (#5, #19; Gallou 2018).

**3. What the skin-colour convention means.** The opened sources record only that it is a convention and that it has exceptions (#20, #21). Its relation to Egyptian practice was seen only in search snippets. Newman (2017, Journal of Mediterranean Archaeology) argues that gender was performed through skin colour, dress and gesture. Matić and Franković note that some costumes appear on both red and white figures. Both were seen only as search snippets (paywall or 403) and are listed as not opened. **Unsettled in this lane.**

**4. Linear A at Akrotiri: local administration or Cretan control?** Michailidou argues for local writing for local purposes by merchant households, possibly without scribes (#63, #65). She cites Renfrew's three options: Cycladic independence with Minoan literacy, Minoan conquest with local autonomy, or direct Minoan rule (#63). The Cretan origin of the sealing motifs and clay (#64) points to correspondence with Crete, not necessarily rule. Boulotis's own publication was not opened.

**5. Where people cooked.** Fixed hearths are missing from the best-preserved houses. The few built hearths in storerooms show little burning, while two small annexe "kitchens" have heavy soot and grease (#39–#43). Birtacha suggests that shared kitchens served several households (#45). The question stays open.

**6. Fish versus meat.** Fish remains are dense across the site (#37), but Trantalidou judges domestic mammals the main source of animal protein (#48). Tuna was thought absent until the kitchen find (#38).

**7. Who the Keftiu were.** Most scholars take Keftiu and Caphtor as Crete (#105, #108). Kyriakidis finds central Crete "very likely" from Amnisos in Amenhotep III's list (#105). Others place Caphtor in southern Asia Minor (#108), and Maxwell-Hyslop doubts that the Mari "weapon of Caphtor" was Cretan (#109). Panagiotopoulos defends the tomb paintings as historical sources, against the view that they are pure convention (#107).

**8. The "Boxing Boys" anomaly.** Ferrence and Bendersky read a medical condition painted from life (#25). Whether it is a deliberate artistic stylisation (they note matching goats on adjoining walls) is left open; no counter-argument was opened.

## Section 6: Game hooks (SUGGESTIONS ONLY: not evidence)

These are design ideas for the project, kept apart from the evidence. Each names the claims it leans on. Anything not graded A must be presented to the viewer as shown or interpreted, not as fact.
- **An empty town, still warm.** No bodies, few valuables, and a tuna steak left in the pan (#24, #38). The player can arrive just after the people have gone. This avoids rendering faces from paintings and fits the evidence that the people fled.
- **Sound bed from grade-A sources:** querns grinding (#45), loom weights clacking (#11), sheep, goats, pigs and cattle (#47), dogs and mice in storerooms (#103), and metal hammering (#53). Instruments (lyre, paired pipes, sistrum, triton trumpet) should be grade B or C and labelled. A seven-string lyre and a triton blast are the best supported (#95, #99). No drums (#100).
- **The Delta merchants' house.** Scale pans, a West Syrian mina, a lead half-talent weight, Cretan-sealed parcels and a Linear A tally (#54, #58, #64). The signs can be shown as signs, never "read" aloud or translated (#70).
- **Kitchen as a set piece.** Build Pillar Shaft 65N to the measurements in Section 3: two-storey hearth, blocked-door hearth, flue, eight querns, horseshoe pans, olive-stone and dung fuel (#42–#46).
- **Clothes from real cloth.** Use the measured thread counts and fringe and tassel details (#7–#9) as the "close-up truth" under painted costume colours. Saffron yellow, red and murex purple are supported (#5, #15).
- **Xeste 3 as a place of ceremony, with the meaning left open.** Show the building, benches, lustral basin and paintings (#71–#75). A viewer panel can set out the competing readings (Section 5.1) rather than choose one.
- **Hair as an age cue for any figure shown** (#16–#19), with a note that this follows painted conventions. Skin colour follows the convention only where the scene recreates a painting, and says so (Historical Standard §4.5).
- **Names screen.** Explain that the island's own name is unknown, that "Thera", "Santorini" and "Akrotiri" are later or modern, and that Egypt's word "Keftiu" refers to Crete (#104, #105).
