## How grades and dates are applied in this lane (read first)

- **A:** excavated at Akrotiri or elsewhere on Thera in the final (Late Cycladic I) destruction context, or a measurement of the eruption deposits on Thera, as the excavators or volcanologists report it.
- **B:** an interpretation of Theran evidence that specialists debate (intervals, evacuation, season); a model built on Theran deposits (plume heights, volumes); evidence found off Thera (tsunami deposits on Crete and in Anatolia, distal ash).
- **C:** modern analogue eruptions, ancient texts about other eruptions, hypotheses without cited evidence, modern population estimates, and definitions.

**Dates.** "E" in the Phase column means the eruption: the end of Late Cycladic I at Akrotiri, late (not final) Late Minoan IA on Crete. Its absolute date is not re-argued here. Following X1 (#13, #14, Summary 1–3): **high (radiocarbon) chronology** c. 1610–1600 BCE (Manning 2022: 1609–1560 BCE at 95.4%, IntCal20); **low (Egyptian-linked) chronology** c. 1560–1500 BCE or later (Pearson et al. 2023 favour the mid-16th century; the Egyptian-linked position is c. 1525–1490 BCE). Intervals before and after the eruption ("E − months") are relative and hold in either chronology. Where a source gives its own absolute date, it is quoted with its curve if the source names one.

## Section 1: Summary

1. **There were at least two earthquakes, and the town was rebuilt after the first.** The Greek Ministry of Culture describes a mid-17th-century BC seismic disaster at the start of Late Cycladic I, followed by rapid rebuilding. A second earthquake struck the town at its height (#4, #5). The second earthquake is the one before the eruption. It left a layer of building debris over the Minoan ground surface (#1).
2. **The last weeks were spent clearing and repairing, and the work was unfinished.** The evidence includes:
   - rubble and earth heaped in the main streets behind dry-stone walls, with grooved "demolition" stones on top (#10, #11);
   - doors walled up on upper floors and new walls built (#9);
   - beds, pots and baskets carried outside, known from their casts in the pumice (#9);
   - an upper room half plastered and painted, with pots of dried plaster and paint beside it (#13);
   - debris still lying in the street south of Xeste 4 (#12).
3. **The town was emptied in an orderly way, apart from heavy and bulky goods.** No bodies and almost no gold or silver were found. Doors were shut, and their wooden leaves left imprints in the ash. Bronze tools and vessels, hoards, balances, silver and copper "money" rings and hundreds of pots stayed behind (#17–#23, #28).
4. **How long passed between the earthquake and the eruption is the lane's biggest open question.** Estimates are: "a few score of days" (Ministry, #6); "a few months" (Doumas 1983, #16); "years, perhaps two or three decades" (Marthari, #16). The thin first ash lay exposed for 2–24 months, or "a few months, no more than a year", or "days to weeks", depending on the author (#42–#44).
5. **Where the people went is not settled.** One reading has them evacuating the island early (Cioni et al., #3). The other has those still in town fleeing west on the island and dying there, because there was no time to sail (Doumas 2019; the Ministry page, #6, #31). No source opened here traces refugees anywhere.
6. **The eruption had five steps.** In order (#41, #49–#62):
   - **Phase 0:** a small precursory ash fall, with a plume of about 8.5 km that blew south. It left about 3 cm at Akrotiri and up to 20 cm in southern Thera.
   - **Phase 1:** the Plinian pumice fall. Its plume rose from about 12 km to a **peak of 28.4 ± 1.0 km** (revised down from the older 36 ± 5 km) as the wind swung from south-southeast to northeast.
   - **Phase 2:** surges.
   - **Phase 3:** cool pyroclastic flows that built a tuff cone filling the caldera.
   - **Phase 4:** hot pyroclastic flows, caldera collapse, then rain-driven lahars, which cut through the buried town.
7. **Phase 1 lasted "several hours".** The whole sequence took perhaps a day to a few days, and the caldera flooded within about two days of the end (#63, #64, #98). No source gives each phase's duration in hours.
8. **The volume is now about 34.5 ± 6.8 km³ dense-rock equivalent (DRE), roughly 73–84 km³ bulk** (Karstens et al. 2023, #65). Older estimates run from 19 to 86 km³ DRE (#67, #68). On the USGS volume scale that makes it VEI 6 (INFERENCE, #73). Older literature calls it VEI 7 (#70–#72).
9. **The ash fell mainly east-southeast, then northeast.** It reached Crete, Rhodes, southwest Anatolia (4 cm in Lake Gölhisar) and the Black Sea. Only trace amounts reached the Nile Delta (#51, #75–#81).
10. **Tsunamis hit Crete and western Anatolia.** At Palaikastro the run-up was at least 9 m. At Malia the water ran up to 8 m and 400 m inland. At Çeşme four waves came over days to weeks, with victims (#82–#88). The likely source is pyroclastic flows entering the sea, not the caldera collapse (#88).
11. **The season was most likely late spring or summer, and the time of day is unknown.** The arguments come from wind and ash dispersal, a pest beetle in stored pulses, and low pantries (#33, #91–#94).
12. **What a person in Akrotiri would have seen and heard can only be built by analogy (grade C).** The likely signs, from modern eruptions and McCoy's synthesis, are:
   - months of small earthquakes, a sulphur smell and a few centimetres of uplift;
   - a sudden ash fall;
   - weeks of quiet;
   - a sharp earthquake, then a white plume rising to stratospheric height, pumice falling at centimetres a minute, darkness lasting more than a day, lightning and sea-waves (#37, #38, #95–#98).

## Section 4: Assumptions checked

- **T7-1** (a strong earthquake damaged Akrotiri; people cleared and repaired, then left; no bodies in the town): **confirmed, with one change.** There were at least two earthquakes, and the second is the one before the eruption (#4–#6). The earthquake damage, clearing and repair are excavated facts (#1, #9–#13). The departure and the absence of bodies are also confirmed (#17, #28). What is not settled is whether the people left the island: Cioni et al. say they did (#3), while Doumas and the Ministry think the last occupants were caught on the island (#6, #31).
- **T7-2** (a precursory ash fall came days to months before the Plinian phase): **confirmed as to the fall; the interval is disputed.** The estimates are days to weeks (#44), a few months and no more than a year (#43), and 2–24 months (#42).
- **T7-3** (the Plinian column reached about 30–40 km): **partly contradicted.** The current best estimate is a peak of 28.4 ± 1.0 km, or 27 ± 5 km (#51, #53). The older figure was 36 ± 5 km (#52). McCoy's 40 km or more is a narrative figure (#98).
- **T7-4** (late spring or summer, judged from ash dispersal to the east-southeast): **partly confirmed.** Spring or summer is supported by a wind model (#92), by insects (#91) and by pantry levels (#33). McCoy admits late autumn as an alternative on wind grounds alone (#93). The dispersal is southeast in early phase 1, then east and northeast (#51, #75).
- **T7-5** (tsunamis reached the north coast of Crete and western Anatolia): **confirmed** at Palaikastro (#82), Malia (#84) and Çeşme (#85). Didim and Fethiye are reported second-hand (#87).
- **T7-6** (a dense-rock-equivalent volume of several tens of km³, about VEI 6 to 7): **confirmed.** The figure is 34.5 ± 6.8 km³ DRE (#65), within an older range of 19–86 km³ (#67). That is VEI 6 on the USGS volume scale (INFERENCE, #73) and VEI 7 or more in older literature (#70–#72).
- **T7-7** (the eruption created or greatly deepened the northern part of the caldera): **partly confirmed.** The collapse enlarged an older, northern, flooded caldera by several hundred metres, mainly in phase 4 (#62). The collapse volume was 33.1 km³ (#66). Its northern focus is in T1 (T1 #20, #31 and its verification caveat); this lane did not re-open those sources.

## Section 5: Debates

1. **How long between the earthquake and the eruption?**
   - **Short.** The Ministry gives "a few score of days" (#6), Doumas in 1983 gave a few months (#16), and Marinatos at first thought it very short (Rutter, #16).
   - **Long.** Marthari's pottery comparison argues for years, perhaps two or three decades, and Marthari and Palyvou say the repairs needed a good deal of time (#16).
   - X1 (#29) records the same split. INFERENCE: the extent of the systematic demolition, the walled-off rubble heaps and the walls built since (#9–#11, #14) is easier to fit into months or years than into weeks.
2. **Did the clearing follow the earthquake or the precursor eruption?**
   - **After the earthquake.** Michailidou has people return after the earthquake, and the precursory ash then drives them out for good. The thin ash lies directly on the debris and preserves a barley sack lying on it (#9, #17, #25).
   - **After the precursor.** McCoy has people return after the precursor eruption, sweeping ash and pumice into piles, then a fresh large earthquake just before the Plinian phase (#45, #46).
   - These readings imply different last scenes. In the first, the ash-dusted town is already empty. In the second, people are back and working in the ash. Neither source quotes the excavation records directly.
3. **Did the people get away?**
   - **They evacuated the island.** Cioni et al. say the earthquakes forced an early evacuation (#3), and Rutter reads the evidence as ample warning (#28).
   - **They were caught on the island.** Doumas (2019) thinks the last occupants fled west across the island and are buried there (#31), and the Ministry says they had no chance to leave (#6).
   - Popular claims that refugees went to Crete were found only in non-scholarly pages and are not used. **Could not verify** any destination.
4. **How long was the precursory phase?** Estimates are days to weeks (Karstens, #44), a few months and no more than a year (McCoy after Heiken and McCoy 1990, #43), and 2–24 months (Rutter, estimator unnamed, #42). The no-erosion argument (#43) rules out a wet winter in between. It does not rule out a short interval.
5. **How high was the Plinian column?** The older estimate is 36 ± 5 km (Sigurdsson et al. 1990, #52). The newer one, from lithic isopleths at 43–44 sites, is 28.4 ± 1.0 km (Myers et al. 2021, #51, #53). Myers et al. suggest that the difference reflects where the samples were taken.
6. **How big was the eruption?**
   - **Large.** Johnston et al. 2014 give 78–86 km³ DRE, based on an assumed thick infill of the caldera (#68). McCoy gives VEI > 7, with up to 100 km³ bulk or more (#70).
   - **Smaller.** Karstens et al. 2023 give 34.5 ± 6.8 km³ DRE. They map a much thinner infill and a smaller marine ignimbrite than Sigurdsson et al. 2006, and their collapse volume (33.1 km³) agrees independently with their deposit total (#65–#67).
   - The 2023 figures are the most recent and the only ones checked against two independent methods.
7. **Which wind level carried which lobe?** Karstens et al. put the Plinian fall on stratospheric winds to the southeast and the co-ignimbrite fall on tropospheric winds to the northeast (#75). McCoy has it the other way round (#76). Both agree that the main axis runs east-southeast to east-northeast.
8. **What season was it?**
   - **Summer, after the harvest.** This rests on insects in the stored pulses (#91).
   - **Spring or summer.** This rests on the wind model (#92).
   - **Late spring or early summer, or late autumn.** McCoy's wind analogy allows both (#93).
   - **May.** Ntokos 2025 (Quaternary International 735: 109838) argues for May. It was **not opened**: only its Crossref record was seen. The May claim rests on a press report that could not be loaded, so it grades nothing here.
   - **Time of day: could not verify**; no source opened gives one.
9. **How much ash fell on Crete?** Estimates run from about 5 cm (older extrapolation, #80) and "little" (Johnston 2012, #92) to more than 15 cm (McCoy, #81). Ash on open ground was quickly eroded, which explains why so little survives (#80, #81).
10. **What made the tsunamis?** Nomikou et al. argue for pyroclastic flows entering the sea in phases 3–4, not the caldera collapse, which happened while the caldera was dry (#88). McCoy and Bruins also allow caldera collapse and landslides (#87, #90). The size of the source wave in the Palaikastro inverse model (+35 m) may be an overestimate (#88).

## Section 6: Game hooks (suggestions only, not evidence)

These are design ideas. Each needs a liberty in `docs/HISTORICAL_STANDARD.md` §6 where it departs from the evidence.

- **The "now" of the world.** The scene could sit in the cleared town after the second earthquake. Rubble walls would line the streets, with grooved stones on top. Beds and baskets would stand outside, and a half-plastered upper room would have its paint pots. Doors would be walled up (#9–#13). It would be summer, with pantries low (#33, #91).
- **Make the open questions into settings rather than facts.** There could be a "short warning" and a "long warning" version of the last weeks, matching Debates 1, 2 and 4. Neither should be presented as settled.
- **A timeline mode** could run in steps:
  1. Precursory signs over months, all grade C (#37).
  2. A brief ash fall, about 3 cm, blowing south (#41, #42).
  3. Quiet.
  4. A sharp quake (#46).
  5. A white plume rising from about 12 to 28 km while the wind turns from south-southeast to northeast, with grey pumice giving way to white (#50, #51).
  6. Darkness, then surges, then lahars (#56, #61).
  7. The sea breaking into the dry caldera within about two days (#63).
- **Sound design** can draw on grade-C analogues only: tremor, cannon-like reports, the hiss of falling pumice, and thunder from electrified plumes (#96, #98). Label it as analogue in the world's notes.
- **Do not show bodies in Akrotiri.** None have been found (#28). Doumas's "fled west" (#31) could appear only as a marked hypothesis.
- **Tsunami scenes on Crete or at Çeşme** belong in the later multi-site world. The Çeşme sequence of four waves over days to weeks, with people searching between them (#85), is the best-documented human story of the event.
